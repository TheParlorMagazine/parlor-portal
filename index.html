Community guidelines

Welcome to The Parlor forum — a space for thoughtful dialogue, critical thinking, and community-building across difference. This is not a free-for-all. It is a curated space grounded in respect, rigor, and care.

By participating, you agree to the following:

1. Speak with intention, not impulse

This is not a reaction-driven space. Take a moment before posting. Aim to contribute something meaningful — whether that's a question, perspective, or lived experience.

Low-effort, inflammatory, or attention-seeking posts may be removed.

2. Disagree thoughtfully

Disagreement is welcome. Dismissiveness is not.

  • Engage ideas, not people
  • No personal attacks, sarcasm aimed at individuals, or bad-faith arguments
  • Avoid pile-ons or targeting behavior
3. No hate, harassment, or dehumanization

Zero tolerance for:

  • Racism, sexism, homophobia, transphobia, xenophobia, ableism
  • Dehumanizing language or coded hostility
  • Dog whistles or "plausible deniability" bigotry

This includes both explicit and subtle forms.

4. Protect lived experience

Many members share personal, political, or vulnerable experiences.

  • Do not invalidate or dismiss someone's lived reality
  • Do not demand emotional labor (e.g., "educate me")
  • Engage with care, especially on topics like trauma, identity, and violence
5. No misinformation or bad-faith framing
  • Do not intentionally spread false or misleading information
  • Avoid conspiracy framing without credible sources
  • Be open to correction

We value critical thinking — but it must be grounded in reality.

6. Stay on topic and add value
  • Keep posts relevant to the thread
  • Avoid derailing conversations
  • Don't dominate discussions or repost the same arguments across threads
7. No self-promotion or spam
  • No unsolicited promotion, links, or advertising
  • Sharing your work is allowed only when directly relevant and not excessive
8. Respect privacy
  • Do not share personal information (yours or others')
  • Do not repost content from this forum outside the platform without consent
9. Moderation is active and intentional

Moderators may remove content, lock threads, or issue warnings or remove members. Decisions are made to protect the integrity of the space — not to censor disagreement.

10. This is a community, not an audience

You are not here to perform. You are here to participate. Thoughtfulness, curiosity, and accountability are valued more than being "right."

The Samizdat Underground: Literature as Survival — Václav Havel, 1978
›

I've been sitting with the line about samizdat being "simply what happens when people refuse to stop reading." There's something quietly radical in that framing that matters for how we think about independent publishing right now.

Havel doesn't romanticise resistance. He describes it as ordinary — something that happens because people are readers, and readers read. The political dimension is almost incidental. They weren't dissidents first; they were readers first.

I keep wondering what the contemporary equivalent is. If the scarcity that made samizdat meaningful is gone — does deliberately choosing to read something carefully, in a particular community, still have any political valence?

Resistance & the Printed Word
The Samizdat Underground: Literature as Survival
Václav Havel  ·  1978  ·  14 pages
Editor's note
"Havel wrote this from inside Czechoslovakia's grip — when the act of printing and sharing a text was itself a criminal offence. I keep coming back to the idea that samizdat wasn't just a workaround. It was a statement about what literature is for."
— Parlor Editors, April 2026

There is something peculiarly modern about the idea that literature can be illegal. Not the book itself — books have been burned since there were books — but the act of writing, copying, passing hand to hand. Under totalitarianism, the typewriter becomes a weapon. The carbon copy becomes contraband.

Samizdat — the word comes from sam (self) and izdat (to publish) — was never a movement. It had no manifesto, no headquarters, no funding. It was simply what happened when people refused to stop reading and writing. A manuscript would be typed, six or eight copies at a time, the carbon paper worn thin by repetition. Each reader became a publisher: you read it, you copied it, you passed it on.

What interests me is not the heroism of this — though there was heroism — but its ordinariness. The people who copied and passed samizdat were not, in the main, dissidents by temperament. They were readers. They simply found themselves in circumstances where reading had become an act of resistance, and they continued to read...
